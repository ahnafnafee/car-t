The corresponding termination and out-of-specification (OOS) rates decreased from 6.8 % to 0.9 %, and from 7.6 % to 3.7 %, respectively. id="sec1"><label>1</label><title>Introduction</title><p id="p0035">Tisagenlecleucel, an autologous CD19-directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, was approved in Japan by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare for the treatment of children and young adults (aged ≤25 years) with relapsed/refractory (r/r) B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) and adults with r/r diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) in March 2019, as well as for r/r follicular lymphoma in August 2022 [<xref rid="bib2" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>]. The manufacturing process of tisagenlecleucel is generally complex, involving leukapheresis to collect peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients [<xref rid="bib3" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>]. These PBMCs are then cryopreserved within 24 h and transported to a manufacturing facility. At the manufacturing facility, the cryopreserved PBMCs are used to produce tisagenlecleucel through various steps, including T-cell enrichment and activation, transduction of the lentiviral vector containing the anti-CD19 chimeric antigen receptor transgene, activation with anti-CD3/CD28 antibody–coated beads, cell culture expansion, washing, and formulation of viable cells into a cryopreservation medium. The final cryopreserved product is then shipped back to the treatment centers for infusion [<xref rid="bib3" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>].</p><p id="p0040">Since CAR T-cell products are manufactured from cells collected from patients, these may fail to reach patients for several reasons. Disease progression and patient health deterioration may lead to the inability to provide a CAR T-cell product in a timely manner. The CAR T-cell product order may be canceled before or after the manufacturing at the institution's request if the patient is in a condition that prevents the infusion of the product, such as disease deterioration, termed as cancellation. In other cases, manufacturing of the CAR T-cell product is terminated for reasons such as insufficient T-cell expansion, referred to as manufacturing termination. In both cases, patients are deprived of CAR T-cell therapy. Even after successful manufacturing, the final CAR T-cell product may occasionally fail to meet all the approved commercial release specifications and be referred as an “out-of-specification (OOS)” product. Such OOS products are administered at the physician's discretion for patients with severe or life-threatening conditions who require urgent treatment and have exhausted all other options, in accordance with health authority guidelines. This is because an OOS product may still have therapeutic effects due to the unique mechanism of action inherent to CAR T-cell therapy [<xref rid="bib4" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>,<xref rid="bib5" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>]. Since the approval of tisagenlecleucel, the primary goal has been to upscale manufacturing capacity and continuously improve manufacturing efficiencies to increase patient access to CAR T-cell therapy worldwide [<xref rid="bib3" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>]. Previous studies have reported process improvements across manufacturing facilities, which have contributed to improved commercial manufacturing experience of tisagenlecleucel for patients in the United States (US) and Europe over the years [<xref rid="bib6" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>,<xref rid="bib7" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>]. This report presents our accrued experience over a 4-year journey of optimizing the commercial tisagenlecleucel manufacturing process and shows how these changes have improved the manufacturing success metrics for patients in Japan.</p></sec><sec id="sec2"><label>2</label><title>Methods</title><sec id="sec2.1"><label>2.1</label><title>Global manufacturing of tisagenlecleucel</title><p id="p0045">Novartis has a significant global commercial manufacturing presence, with two manufacturing facilities (US and Switzerland). As of December 31, 2023, approximately 630 certified treatment network centers were strategically located in 35 countries worldwide, including 47 in Japan, to meet the demand of patients in need of CAR T-cell treatment. Tisagenlecleucel has been manufactured for over 11,400 patients globally, including 1300 patients in Japan.</p></sec><sec id="sec2.2"><label>2.2</label><title>Tisagenlecleucel manufacturing experience</title><p id="p0050">Tisagenlecleucel manufacturing records at Novartis were used and aggregated data presented based on definitions as described below. Commercial tisagenlecleucel orders from Japan that started manufacturing from August 2019 to July 2023 were included. Orders cancelled before the initiation of manufacturing were excluded. The data of cancellation after shipment were collected from cancellation forms obtained from institutions. Data were collected and summarized annually, starting from year 1 (August 2019 to July 2020) to year 4 (August 2022 to July 2023) to assess the yearly changes. Descriptive statistics were used to express the data as percentages or means or in the form of visual summaries such as bar graphs or line charts. No formal analysis was performed.</p><sec id="sec2.2.1"><label>2.2.1</label><title>Key definitions</title><p id="p0055">
<list list-type="simple" id="ulist0015"><list-item id="u0035"><label>•</label><p id="p0060">Manufacturing success rate (MSR) was defined as the proportion of batches for which the manufactured product met the health authority (HA) release criteria among the total number of batches for which tisagenlecleucel manufacturing was initiated (excluding cancelled orders during manufacturing).</p></list-item><list-item id="u0040"><label>•</label><p id="p0065">Shipment success rate (SSR) was defined as the proportion of batches for which the manufactured product (meeting HA release criteria + out-of-specification [OOS] provisioned based on a favorable benefit-risk profile) was available for shipment among the total number of batches for which tisagenlecleucel manufacturing was initiated (excluding cancelled orders during manufacturing).</p></list-item><list-item id="u0045"><label>•</label><p id="p0070">Termination rate was defined as the proportion of batches for which a manufactured product could not be produced (e.g., due to low growth of cells) among the total number of batches for which tisagenlecleucel manufacturing was initiated (excluding cancelled orders during manufacturing).</p></list-item><list-item id="u0050"><label>•</label><p id="p0075">OOS rate was defined as the proportion of batches for which the manufactured product did not meet the HA release criteria among the total number of batches for which tisagenlecleucel manufacturing was initiated (excluding cancelled orders during manufacturing).</p></list-item><list-item id="u0055"><label>•</label><p id="p0080">Cancellation rate was defined as the proportion of batches for which the product was cancelled due to institution's request after starting the manufacturing among the total number of batches for which tisagenlecleucel manufacturing was initiated. These also include cancellations after institutions receive the manufactured product.</p></list-item></list>
</p></sec></sec></sec><sec id="sec3"><label>3</label><title>Results</title><sec id="sec3.1"><label>3.1</label><title>Manufacturing success metrics</title><p id="p0085">The annual changes in manufacturing success metrics are shown in <xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">Fig. 1</xref>. In Japan, over the last 4 years (i.e., year 1 to year 4), MSR and SSR improved from 85.6 % to 95.3 % and from 92.4 % to 98.4 %, respectively (<xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">Fig. 1</xref>A–B). The corresponding termination and OOS rates decreased from 6.8 % to 0.9 % and from 7.6 % to 3.7 %, respectively (<xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">Fig. 1</xref>C–D). In year 4 (between August 2022 and July 2023), the MSR, SSR, termination rate, and overall OOS rates were 95.3 %, 98.4 %, 0.9 %, and 3.7 %, respectively. These improvements were attributed to optimizing the manufacturing capabilities and enhancements with hospitals focusing on the optimized timing of leukapheresis. The number of annual manufacturing has also increased significantly over 4 years, indicating the success of upscaling.<fig id="fig1" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 1</label><caption><p>Evolution of MSR (A), SSR (B), termination (C), OOS (D), and cancellation rates (E) from August 2019 to July 2023. The rates of product cancellation by institutions requested after manufacturing initiation increased from 1.7 % in year 1 to 5.1 % in year 4. Major reasons for cancellations were death or deteriorated health before infusion as reported in the cancel forms received from institutions. In most cases (86.3 %), the cancellations occurred after the product was shipped to the institutions. In contrast to MSR improvements, the cancellation rate gradually increased from year 1 to year 4 with an increase in the number of CAR T-cell batches. It was considered that in-hospital capacity and increasing waiting time before infusion were related to this event in addition to patient selection.</p></sec><sec id="sec3.2"><label>3.2</label><title>Manufacturing process improvements</title><p id="p0095">Since the launch of tisagenlecleucel, Novartis has performed continuous evaluation and improvement in the manufacturing and analytical processes to optimize the manufacturing capabilities. The key process improvements include the use of 5 % plasma-derived human AB serum as an alternative serum source (<xref rid="fig2" ref-type="fig">Fig. 2</xref>A) and a simplified sample preparation for final product cell viability testing (<xref rid="fig2" ref-type="fig">Fig. 2</xref>B).<fig id="fig2" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 2</label><caption><p>Manufacturing process improvements. Additionally, the termination and OOS rates decreased from 6.0 % to 1.9 % and from 6.1 % to 3.5 %, respectively, with the viability OOS rates having decreased from 2.8 % to 0.2 % (<xref rid="fig3" ref-type="fig">Fig. 3</xref> C-D).<fig id="fig3" position="float" orientation="portrait"><label>Fig. 3</label><caption><p>MSR (A), SSR (B), termination rate (C), and overall OOS rates (D) before and after process improvements in Japan. The tisagenlecleucel OOS batches were provided under a clinical trial (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" ext-link-type="pmc:clinical-trial" xlink:href="NCT04094311">NCT04094311</ext-link>) in Japan [<xref rid="bib8" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>]. From year 1 to year 4, 42 patients received infusion with OOS tisagenlecleucel (6 patients with B-ALL and 36 patients with DLBCL) in 18 of 44 certified treatment centers.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec4"><label>4</label><title>Discussion</title><p id="p0110">This report suggests the accrued experience from a 4-year journey of optimizing the commercial tisagenlecleucel manufacturing and implementing manufacturing process improvements in Japan. These improvements have significantly increased the robustness of the manufacturing and testing processes, and the reliability of delivering tisagenlecleucel to patients in need of treatment. Over the past 4 years in Japan, the SSR improved to over 98 % and the MSR improved to over 95 %, consistent with previously reported experiences in the US and Europe [<xref rid="bib6" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>,<xref rid="bib7" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>]. These improvements are likely the result of continuous evaluation and enhancement of the manufacturing and analytical processes, upscaling the manufacturing capabilities, increased expertise in selecting appropriate patients with right conditions, and optimizing leukapheresis collection and cell processing at the right time for patients.</p><p id="p0115">Since the launch of tisagenlecleucel, major manufacturing process changes have not been implemented in Japan until 2022. However, continuous evaluation of each product and appropriate communication with treatment centers have contributed to the manufacturing improvement. In addition, two key processes introduced in 2022 have enhanced the robustness of the manufacturing and testing processes that reduced the rates of OOS and manufacturing terminations. First, the use of an alternative serum source improved process robustness, showing a trend toward enhanced growth and higher peak cell counts. Second, a simplified sample preparation procedure for final product cell counts and viability measurement better reflected the viability of the final product at infusion. These process changes have led to the decrease of termination rates from 6.0 % to 1.9 % and OOS rates from 6.1 % to 3.5 %, which resulted in improved MSR (&gt;95 %) and SSR (&gt;98 %) in year 4.</p><p id="p0120">The patient- and disease-specific variables associated with the failure to manufacture commercial CAR T-cell therapies for patients include factors at apheresis predictive of manufacturing failure, such as poor performance status, elevated levels of lactate dehydrogenase, C-reactive protein, ferritin, and reduced counts of absolute lymphocyte/monocyte counts, hemoglobin, and platelets [<xref rid="bib9" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>]. A nationwide survey in Japan conducted by Jo and colleagues (2023) identified several risk factors for CAR T-cell manufacturing failure among patients with DLBCL. These include prior use of bendamustine with short washout periods before apheresis, low platelet counts, and low CD4/CD8 ratios in peripheral blood at apheresis [<xref rid="bib10" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>]. A study conducted by Fried and colleagues (2022) revealed that having received ≥4 prior therapies or previous bendamustine exposure are risk factors for OOS tisagenlecleucel [<xref rid="bib11" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>]. Cohet and colleagues (2023) also reported that high TNC, CD3<sup>+</sup> cells and neutrophil concentration in the apheresis product might be predictive factors of failures and/or OOS of commercial CAR T-cell manufacturing [<xref rid="bib12" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>]. These results suggest that avoiding risk factors and adopting risk-adapted strategies such as early referral could optimize CAR T-cell therapy [<xref rid="bib11" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="bib12" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>]. Additionally, a study by Smrekar T <italic toggle="yes">et al.,</italic> (2022) evaluated the impact of using historically collected leukapheresis material on the outcomes of tisagenlecleucel manufacturing. The findings revealed a decrease in the median time between order submission and product pickup. Interestingly, a marginally higher tisagenlecleucel MSR was observed when the leukapheresis material was collected more than 60 days before order submission when compared with that in products manufactured from leukapheresis material collected after the order was placed [<xref rid="bib13" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. A prospective study by Sharon and colleagues (2023) evaluated the potential benefit of early apheresis (early group, n = 22) versus standard apheresis (i.e., at the second relapse and beyond, standard group, n = 23) on clinical outcomes of CAR T-cell–treated patients with r/r DLBCL. The early group showed more naïve T cells, better <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> T-cell functionality, and lower exhaustion profiles. Although these improvements did not lead to significantly better clinical outcomes, there was a trend toward improved overall and progression-free survival [<xref rid="bib14" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>]. In summary, these findings suggest that, in addition to process and analytical improvements in manufacturing, increased physicians’ experience, identification and mitigation of manufacturing failure risk factors, and optimized timing of leukapheresis likely contributed to improve manufacturing success and clinical outcomes [<xref rid="bib6" ref-type="bibr">[5]</xref>, <xref rid="bib7" ref-type="bibr">[6]</xref>, <xref rid="bib8" ref-type="bibr">[7]</xref>, <xref rid="bib9" ref-type="bibr">[8]</xref>, <xref rid="bib10" ref-type="bibr">[9]</xref>, <xref rid="bib11" ref-type="bibr">[10]</xref>, <xref rid="bib12" ref-type="bibr">[11]</xref>, <xref rid="bib13" ref-type="bibr">[12]</xref>, <xref rid="bib14" ref-type="bibr">[13]</xref>, <xref rid="bib15" ref-type="bibr">[14]</xref>].</p><p id="p0125">Despite the favorable outcomes seen with CAR T-cell therapy, multiple barriers continue to hinder patients’ access. In our study, 5 % of patients did not receive the therapy due to product cancellation in the year 4. There has been a gradual increase in product cancellations from institutions after the initiation of manufacturing, primarily due to patient death or disease deterioration. In most cases, these cancellations occurred after the product had been shipped to the institutions. Since tisagenlecleucel is indicated for patients with life-threatening conditions, in some instances, delays in treatment could lead to disease progression. In Japan, an upward trend in the cancellation rate has been observed, correlating with the increase in manufacturing volume. This trend could be partially attributed to the extended waiting times for infusion, a consequence of limited infusion-site capacities. Currently, several CAR T-cell therapies have been approved in Japan, and numerous clinical trials are underway for various indications [<xref rid="bib2" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>]. Constraints in human resources and hospital bed capacities have resulted in extended waiting times at some centers. Therefore, there is a pressing need for increased resource availability to optimize the delivery of these potentially life-saving therapies.</p><p id="p0130">Although the incidence rates of OOS tisagenlecleucel have considerably decreased since its approval in Japan, the possibility of OOS persists owing to the inherent nature of cellular therapy. As OOS events are unpredictable, all tisagenlecleucel treatment centers must participate in a clinical trial if an OOS event occurs, which present challenges for both treatment centers and sponsor companies. In Japan, patients receive OOS tisagenlecleucel in a phase-3b trial (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" ext-link-type="pmc:clinical-trial" xlink:href="NCT04094311">NCT04094311</ext-link>) [<xref rid="bib8" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>]. Initial results from this trial, involving patients enrolled under the original protocol (N = 29), showed that the safety and efficacy profile of OOS tisagenlecleucel appears comparable that of in-specification tisagenlecleucel in clinical and real-world studies [<xref rid="bib8" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>,<xref rid="bib16" ref-type="bibr">[15]</xref>, <xref rid="bib17" ref-type="bibr">[16]</xref>, <xref rid="bib18" ref-type="bibr">[17]</xref>]. These findings, alongside other trials investigating the clinical outcomes of OOS CAR T cells, help establish the risk-benefit profile and could enhance patient access to these possibly life-saving therapies [<xref rid="bib4" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>,<xref rid="bib5" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>,<xref rid="bib17" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>].</p><p id="p0135">A few limitations of our analysis must be noted. The current analysis does not include clinical data or cellular characteristics such as cell components of apheresis or final products, as these factors may be associated with improved manufacturing success. The patient's condition at the time of product order may associated with cancellation. By optimizing the timing of patient selection, considering waiting times, can help maintain patient's condition until infusion and potentially reduce cancellations. Additionally, our reported cancellation rates do not account for patients for whom an order was placed but subsequently canceled before manufacturing was initiated, as these instances have less impact on manufacturing. However, in practice, there are instances where product orders are canceled before manufacturing is initiated, which may still impose burden on medical institutions and transportation.</p></sec><sec id="sec5"><label>5</label><title>Conclusions</title><p id="p0140">In conclusion, the manufacturing success of commercial tisagenlecleucel has improved since its approval in Japan at least partially by process improvements. Although OOS and manufacturing failure rates have improved, the cancellation or the decision to not proceed with CAR T-cell therapy remains a significant area for improvement in terms of patient access. Moreover, there are still opportunities for improvement in terms of the timing of infusion of CAR T-cell therapy (reducing waiting time to infusion through increasing optimal in-hospital capacity and further manufacturing improvement and simplifying the pathway of providing OOS products). Future improvements are expected to occur with cooperation among industry, academia, and government to ensure that CAR T-cell therapy is used appropriately in patients requiring this treatment in Japan.</p></sec><sec id="sec7"><title>Author contributions</title><p id="p0150">All authors contributed to study conception and design; acquisition, analysis, and interpretation of data; and drafting or critically revising the manuscript.
